1.1Background & technologies

Recognising people by what they are

A biometric is a measurable physical or behavioural characteristic that can identify a person automatically. It is the “something you are” factor, alongside “something you know” (password) and “something you have” (token).

PHYSIOLOGICAL

Fingerprint, face, iris, retina, hand geometry, palm vein, DNA. Body structure, usually stable over time.

BEHAVIOURAL

Voice, signature, keystroke rhythm, gait. How a person acts, so it varies more with mood, health and age.

Active biometrics need the user to cooperate: press a finger on a sensor, look into an iris camera, speak a passphrase.
Passive biometrics are captured without any action: a face in a CCTV frame, gait from a distance, voice during a normal call.

1.4Iris recognition

1.5Strong authentication & privacy

Why biometrics, and what can go wrong

NEED FOR STRONG AUTHENTICATION

Passwords are reused, shared and phished. Biometrics bind an action to a real person and cannot be forgotten. Combined with another factor they resist most account-takeover attacks.

PRIVACY AND POLICY

A biometric cannot be changed if leaked. Systems need consent, purpose limitation, template protection (encryption, cancelable biometrics) and minimal retention. India’s Aadhaar and the 2017 Puttaswamy privacy judgment are key local examples.

2.1History & description

Ridges, valleys and three families

Herschel used handprints on contracts in 1850s India; Galton showed prints are unique and permanent; the Henry system, developed in Bengal with Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, organised them for police records. Today’s AFIS systems still use the same vocabulary.

Ridge / valley
Raised skin lines and the gaps between them.
Core
Centre of the innermost loop or whorl.
Delta
Triangular meeting point of three ridge flows.
Minutiae
Local ridge events: ridge ending and bifurcation. The basis of matching.
2.2Processing pipeline

Generate a print, then process it

Pick a pattern class, then step through the pipeline. Each stage is computed live from a ridge-orientation model, exactly the way enhancement algorithms reason about prints.

ENHANCEMENT & MODELING

Estimate the orientation field and ridge frequency in each block, then apply oriented Gabor filters that strengthen ridges along their direction and remove noise.

FEATURE EXTRACTION

Binarise, thin ridges to one pixel, then scan with the crossing-number method: 1 neighbour transition = ridge ending, 3 = bifurcation.

How the print is captured

Quality assessment (e.g. NIST NFIQ) scores clarity, contrast and ridge continuity before enrolment. Low-quality captures are rejected and retaken, which reduces failure-to-enrol and false rejects later.

Detect, find landmarks, compare

A face system detects the face, locates landmarks, turns geometry and texture into a feature vector, then measures distance to enrolled people. Shape the probe face with the sliders, or add pose and lighting noise.

3.2Hand geometry

Scanning and measuring the hand

A camera captures the top and side of a hand placed against guide pegs. The system measures finger lengths and widths, palm width and thickness, then compares those numbers with the enrolled template.

Adaptive classifier: after a successful match the template moves slightly toward the new sample, so slow changes (weight, age) don’t cause rejections.

3.3Visual features & fusion

Feature extraction, classifiers, fusion

VISUAL FEATURE EXTRACTION

Edges, contours, texture (LBP, Gabor), keypoints (SIFT/ORB) and learned CNN features turn pixels into numbers a classifier can use.

TYPES OF ALGORITHM

Nearest-neighbour and template matching, statistical classifiers (Bayes, LDA), SVMs, and neural networks.

Biometric fusion combines evidence from several traits, samples or algorithms. It is covered fully in Unit IV.

4.3Performance evaluation

FAR, FRR, FTE and EER

Genuine and impostor comparisons produce two score distributions. Wherever you place the threshold, one error grows as the other shrinks.

—FAR

2.0%FTE

FAR
Impostor attempts accepted ÷ all impostor attempts. A security failure.
FRR
Genuine attempts rejected ÷ all genuine attempts. A usability failure.
FTE
Users who cannot be enrolled ÷ all users (worn fingerprints, eye conditions).
EER
The point where FAR = FRR. Lower EER means a better system overall.
4.4Memory requirement

Memory requirement and allocation

Template size times population sets storage needs, and decides whether matching can run on a card, a phone or must use a server.

FIG 4.4 Template storage calculator

Typical sizes: fingerprint minutiae ≈ 250 B–1 KB, IrisCode 512 B, face embedding 512 floats ≈ 2 KB, raw fingerprint image ≈ 100 KB. Smart cards hold a few KB of free memory.

5.2Expectation–Maximization

Fitting a Gaussian Mixture Model

EM fits a mixture of Gaussians to data whose group labels are unknown. The E-step estimates how much each point belongs to each Gaussian; the M-step moves the Gaussians to fit those weights. Repeat until stable. This is how classic speaker models are trained.

5.3Securing the transaction

Where should matching happen?

The matching location decides where the template lives, what travels over the network, and what an attacker must break.

FIG 5.3 Matching location and data flow

Matching location

TRUSTING THE TRANSACTION

Encrypt and sign every message, use liveness detection at the sensor, protect stored templates, use time-stamps or nonces to block replay, and log every decision.

AUTHENTICATION METHODS

Fingerprint (minutiae matching), face (embedding distance), hand geometry (measurement distance), and combinations of these with cards or PINs.
